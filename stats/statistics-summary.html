★★★★★

★★★★★

Sampling Methods

≈— marks · 5 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

Why the way you choose a sample matters

★★★★★

In plain words: a sample is useful only if it looks like the population. If you ask only your friends, you learn about your friends, not about everyone. So statisticians use random methods, where chance — not you — decides who is in the sample.

  • Parameters (μ, σ, p) describe the population and are usually unknown
  • Pollster: knows answers are agree/disagree (binomial) but not p, the true share who agree
  • Agronomist: knows wheat yield is roughly normal but not μ or σ
  • The sampling plan (experimental design) decides how much information you get and how reliable it is

Simple random sampling

★★★★★

In plain words: every possible group of n people has the same chance of being picked — like drawing names from a hat.

89 students, need a group of 5:
1. Number students 01 to 89
2. Read 5 pairs of digits from a random number table
3. If 90–99 or 00 comes up, skip it and read another
4. The 5 students with those numbers form the group
Use a computer or random number tableProblems: non-response, undercoverage, wording bias

Describing Data: Spread, Parameters, Statistics, Estimation

≈— marks · 4 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

Measures of spread: range, mean deviation, variance, SD

★★★★★

In plain words: the mean tells you the centre. Spread tells you how far the values sit from that centre. If spread (variance) is high, the mean is a poor summary of the data — your notes say 'variance high, mean not good'.

Range = largest − smallest        (a crude measure)
Mean deviation = (1/N) Σ |xᵢ − x̄|
Variance σ² = (1/N) Σ (xᵢ − x̄)²     = average squared distance from the mean
Standard deviation σ = √variance
Worked: values 1, 2, 3 → mean 2
Variance = [(1−2)² + (2−2)² + (3−2)²] ÷ 3 = (1 + 0 + 1) ÷ 3 = 2/3
SD = √(2/3) ≈ 0.816

Parameter vs statistic

★★★★★

In plain words: a parameter is a number about the whole population (usually unknown). A statistic is the same kind of number worked out from your sample (known).

Population parameterSample statistic
Meanμx̄
SDσₚ (σ)σₛ (s)
Varianceσₚ²σₛ²
Proportionp (P)p̂ (p)
Going from the known sample to a guess about the unknown population = estimationHypotheses are tested with sample statistics

Sample SD: why divide by n − 1

★★★★★

In plain words: if you divide by n when working out a sample variance, the answer is on average a bit too small. Dividing by n − 1 fixes this, so it is 'unbiased'.

Population SD:  σₚ = √[ (1/N) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² ]
Sample SD:      σₛ = √[ (1/(n−1)) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² ]
With divisor n: E(σₛ²) = ((n−1)/n) σₚ²   → biased (too small)
Multiply by n/(n−1):  (n/(n−1)) × (1/n) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = (1/(n−1)) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²  → unbiased
Sample (n = 2, with replacement) from {1,2,3}x̄
(1,1)1
(1,2) (2,1)1.5
(1,3) (2,2) (3,1)2
(2,3) (3,2)2.5
(3,3)3
Average of all 9 sample means = 2 = μ → E(x̄) = μ: the sample mean is an unbiased estimatorIf σₚ is not given, use σₛ as its estimate
Trap Your notes use capital N for the sample size in this proof. Read it as small n (sample size).

Standard error

★★★★★

In plain words: if you took many samples, their means would differ a little. The standard error is the SD of those sample means. Bigger samples → smaller standard error → more precise.

SE of mean = σ / √n
SE of proportion = √(pq / n)
σ = SD, σ² = variance, σ/√n = standard error

Probability Distributions: Binomial, Poisson, Geometric, Normal

≈— marks · 4 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

Binomial distribution

★★★★★

In plain words: you repeat a yes/no trial n times (toss a coin 10 times). Each trial has success chance p and failure chance q = 1 − p. The binomial gives the chance of exactly x successes.

P(x) = ⁿCₓ pˣ qⁿ⁻ˣ ,   x = 0, 1, 2 … n
Mean = np      SD = √(npq)
n is finitePollster's agree/disagree answers follow a binomial

Poisson distribution

★★★★★

In plain words: when trials are extremely many (n → ∞) and success is very rare (p → 0) but np stays a fixed number λ, the binomial turns into the Poisson — used for counts of rare events (accidents per day).

P(X = x) = e^(−λ) λˣ / x! ,   x = 0, 1, 2 … ∞      λ = np

Geometric distribution

★★★★★

In plain words: you keep tossing a coin and stop the moment you get the first head. The number of tosses needed follows the geometric distribution.

P(first success on trial x) = q^(x−1) p   (Added)

Normal distribution & z-score

★★★★★

In plain words: the normal curve is the symmetric bell shape. Most values sit near the mean; few are far away. A z-score says how many SDs a value is from the mean. Converting to z lets one table (Table 3) give every normal probability.

z = (x − mean) / SD        → standard normal variate (mean 0, SD 1)
Runs from −∞ to +∞, centred at 0
Area rule (Added)Share of values
Within ±1 SD≈ 68%
Within ±2 SD≈ 95%
Within ±3 SD≈ 99.7%

Sampling Distributions & the Central Limit Theorem

≈— marks · 4 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

What a sampling distribution is

★★★★★

In plain words: a statistic (like the sample mean) changes from sample to sample, so it is a random variable. If you list every value it can take and how often, you get its sampling distribution.

Population: 3, 5, 2, 1   → all samples of n = 3, without replacement
3,5,2 → x̄ = 3.33 ; 3,5,1 → 3 ; 3,2,1 → 2 ; 5,2,1 → 2.67
Each x̄ has probability 1/4
Found by maths, by simulation, or by theorems like the Central Limit Theorem

Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

★★★★★

In plain words: even if the population is not bell-shaped, the average of a large random sample behaves like a bell curve. This is why the normal curve appears everywhere in statistics.

If the population has mean μ and SD σ, then for large n:
x̄ ~ approximately Normal(mean = μ, SD = σ/√n)
Sum of n values ~ approximately Normal(mean = nμ, SD = σ√n)
Population shapeHow large must n be?
NormalAny n — x̄ is always normal
Roughly symmetricFairly small n
Skewedn ≥ 30
Die example (fair die, faces 1–6)MeanSD of x̄
n = 1 (flat/uniform)3.51.71
n = 2 (mound-shaped)3.51.71/√2 = 1.21
n = 3 (≈ normal)3.51.71/√3 = 0.987

Sampling distribution of the sample mean

★★★★★
Mean of x̄ = μ        SD of x̄ (standard error) = σ/√n
z = (x̄ − μ) / (σ/√n)
n = 16 from Normal(μ 10, σ 8): P(x̄ > 12) = P(z > (12−10)/(8/4)) = P(z > 1) = 1 − .8413 = .1587
Soda fills: μ 12.1 oz, σ 0.2, 6-pack: P(x̄ < 12) = P(z < (12 − 12.1)/(0.2/√6)) = P(z < −1.22) = .1112

Sampling distribution of the sample proportion

★★★★★

In plain words: p̂ = x/n is the share of 'successes' in your sample (e.g. share of defective cans). For large n it is also roughly normal.

Mean of p̂ = p        SE = √(pq/n)        z = (p̂ − p) / √(pq/n)
Condition: np > 5 and nq > 5
n = 100, p = .4: P(p̂ > .5) = P(z > (.5 − .4)/√(.4×.6/100)) = P(z > 2.04) = 1 − .9793 = .0207
Soda: claim p = .05 underfilled, n = 200 (np = 10, nq = 190 → OK)
P(p̂ > .10) = P(z > (.10 − .05)/√(.05×.95/200)) = P(z > 3.24) = 1 − .9994 = .0006 → very unusual if p really is .05

Hypothesis Testing: The Logic

≈— marks · 5 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

Null and alternative hypotheses

★★★★★

In plain words: a hypothesis test is like a court case. The null hypothesis (H₀) is 'nothing special is happening' — the accused is innocent. The alternative (Hₐ or H₁) is what the researcher suspects. We keep H₀ unless the sample gives strong evidence against it.

H₀ — statistical hypothesisHₐ / H₁ — research hypothesis
Exampleμ_girls = μ_boys (no difference in average marks)μ_girls > μ_boys, or <, or ≠
Always contains'=' sign'>', '<' or '≠'
HₐTest typeRejection area
μ ≠ valueTwo-tailedBoth tails
μ > valueOne-tailed (right)Right tail
μ < valueOne-tailed (left)Left tail

Type I and Type II errors

★★★★★
DecisionH₀ actually trueH₀ actually false
Reject H₀Type I error (probability α)Correct (power = 1 − β)
Do not reject H₀CorrectType II error (probability β)
α = P(reject H₀ | H₀ true) = level of significance (LOS)
β = P(do not reject H₀ | H₀ false)
Power of test = 1 − β   → approaches 1 as β → 0
Common α: 1%, 2%, 5%, 10%
Neyman–Pearson lemma: fix α, then choose the test with the highest powerANOVA problem: α = 1% chosen when a false 'drug works' claim would be costly
Trap Your notes say 'α = consumer risk, β = producer risk'. The standard terms are the other way: α = producer's risk (a good lot is rejected), β = consumer's risk (a bad lot is accepted).
Trap 'α = 1%' does not mean 'H₀ is true'. It means you accept a 1% chance of rejecting a true H₀ — i.e. 99% confidence.

Decision rules: critical value and p-value

★★★★★
CompareDecision
|calculated| > |tabulated| (critical)Reject H₀
|calculated| < |tabulated|Do not reject H₀
p-value ≤ 0.05Reject H₀ at 5%
p-value > 0.05Do not reject H₀ at 5%

Reject H₀ is a strong decision: the sample evidence is statistically significant enough. Do not reject is weaker: we only lack evidence; we never 'prove' H₀.

Trap One line in your notes says 'if p-value > 0.05, reject H₀'. That is wrong. Reject when p ≤ 0.05. A small p-value means the data are unlikely if H₀ were true.

The step-by-step procedure

★★★★★
1. State H₀ and Hₐ (decide one- or two-tailed)
2. Set α (usually 5%)
3. Compute the test statistic (z, t, χ², F) and its degrees of freedom
4. Write the decision rule: reject H₀ if statistic ≥ critical value
5. Decide: reject or do not reject
6. State the result in plain English

Tests for Means: z-test and t-test (worked)

≈— marks · 7 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

Which test: z or t?

★★★★★

In plain words: both compare a sample mean with a value (or with another mean). Use z when the sample is large (n ≥ 30) or the population SD is known. Use t when the sample is small (n < 30) and you must estimate SD from the sample.

Small sample test (n < 30)Large sample test (n ≥ 30)
t-testz-test (normal test)
1. One sample (single mean)1. One sample (single mean)
2. Two independent samples2. Two samples
3. Paired (dependent) t-test3. Proportion test
One-sample t:  t = (x̄ − μ) / (σₛ/√n)       df = n − 1
One-sample z:  z = (x̄ − μ) / (σₚ/√n)
σ/√n is the standard error (SE)

One-sample z-test (worked: heights)

★★★★★
n = 400, x̄ = 67.47", μ = 67.39", σₚ = 1.30", α = 5%
I.   H₀: μ = 67.39   vs   Hₐ: μ ≠ 67.39 (two-tailed)
II.  z = (x̄ − μ) / (σₚ/√n)
III. z = (67.47 − 67.39) / (1.30/20) = 0.08 / 0.065 = 1.23
IV.  z tab (5%, two-tailed) = 1.96 → |1.23| < 1.96
V.   Do not reject H₀: the sample is consistent with a population mean of 67.39"
If population size is unknown, treat it as infinite
Trap Your notes say the sample 'belongs to the population whose mean was 67.47'. The population mean is 67.39; 67.47 is the sample mean.

Finite population correction (worked)

★★★★★

In plain words: when the population is small and known (N), and you sample a big part of it, the SE shrinks. Multiply SE by √((N − n)/(N − 1)).

z = (x̄ − μ) / [ (σₚ/√n) × √((N − n)/(N − 1)) ]
N = 20, μ = 320, σₚ = 75, n = 5, x̄ = 300
H₀: μ = 320  vs  Hₐ: μ ≠ 320
z = (300 − 320) / [ (75/√5) × √(15/19) ] = −20 / 29.8 = −0.67
|0.67| < 1.96 → do not reject H₀: sample mean consistent with population mean at 95% confidence
Trap One line in your notes writes the correction as (N − n)/(n − 1). The correct factor is √((N − n)/(N − 1)), as used in the working.

One-tailed z-test (worked: production process)

★★★★★
μ = 50, σₚ = 2.5, n = 12, x̄ = 48.5, α = 5%
H₀: μ = 50 (process unchanged)   Hₐ: μ < 50 (manager guards against a fall)
z = (48.5 − 50) / (2.5/√12) = −1.5 / 0.722 = −2.08
z tab (5%, one-tailed) = 1.645 → |2.08| > 1.645
Reject H₀: the process mean has fallen
Trap The notes write σ/√5 in one line and label Hₐ as 'H₀: μ < 50'. Use √12 (n = 12) and call μ < 50 the alternative.

One-sample t-test (worked: bag weights)

★★★★★
Values: 578, 572, 570, 568, 572, 578, 570, 572, 596, 544   (n = 10)
H₀: μ = 578   Hₐ: μ ≠ 578
x̄ = 5720/10 = 572
Σ(x − x̄)² = 36+0+4+16+0+36+4+0+576+784 = 1456
σₛ = √(1456/9) = 12.72
t = (572 − 578) / (12.72/√10) = −1.49
t tab (9 df, 5%, two-tailed) = 2.262 → |1.49| < 2.262 → do not reject H₀
Second example: n = 12, x̄ = 6576/12 = 548, H₀: μ = 500, Hₐ: μ > 500
σₛ = √(23979/11) = 46.68
t = (548 − 500) / (46.68/√12) = 3.56

Two-sample z-test (worked)

★★★★★
z = [(x̄₁ − x̄₂) − (μ₁ − μ₂)] / √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂)
Under H₀: μ₁ − μ₂ = 0 → z = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂)
σ's unknown → use sample σₛ₁², σₛ₂²
Same σ for both → z = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / [σ √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)]  (σ unknown → combined σ)
n₁ = 100, x̄₁ = 200, n₂ = 150, x̄₂ = 220, population σ = 11, α = 5%
H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ (same population)   Hₐ: μ₁ ≠ μ₂
z = (200 − 220) / [11 √(1/100 + 1/150)] = −20 / 1.42 = −14.08
|14.08| > 1.96 → reject H₀: the two samples come from different populations

Paired (dependent) t-test

★★★★★

In plain words: use it when the same people are measured twice (before and after a course). Work with the differences D.

t = D̄ / (σ_D/√n)       σ_D = √[ (1/(n−1)) Σ(Dᵢ − D̄)² ]       df = n − 1

Tests for Proportions & Variances (F-test)

≈— marks · 3 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

One-sample proportion test

★★★★★

In plain words: use it for attributes — something present or absent (male/female, defective/good). You compare the sample share with a claimed population share.

z = (p̂ − P) / √(PQ/n)        Q = 1 − P
Births: n = 3232, boys 1705 → p̂ = 0.5275; claim P = 1/2
z = (0.5275 − 0.5) / √(0.5 × 0.5 / 3232) = 3.13 > 1.96 → reject H₀: the data do not support a 50:50 ratio
First class passengers: n = 400, 70 hold first class → p̂ = 0.175; H₀: P = 0.20, Hₐ: P ≠ 0.20
z = (0.175 − 0.20) / √(0.2 × 0.8 / 400) = −0.025 / 0.02 = −1.25
At 10%: z tab = 1.645 → |1.25| < 1.645 → do not reject H₀
Defectives: n = 400, 34 defective → p̂ = 0.085; H₀: P = 10%, Hₐ: P < 10%
z = (0.085 − 0.10) / √(0.10 × 0.90 / 400) = −0.015 / 0.015 = −1.0
At 1% one-tailed: z tab = 2.33 → do not reject H₀

Two-sample proportion test

★★★★★
z = (p₁ − p₂) / √(P₁Q₁/n₁ + P₂Q₂/n₂)
Trap Your notes write the two-sample proportion test with x̄ and σ² terms. That is the formula for two means. For proportions use p and pq, as above.

F-test for equality of variances

★★★★★

In plain words: it checks whether two samples are equally spread out ('homogeneous'). Homogeneous variance means one group is not more variable than the other.

H₀: σ₁² = σ₂²   vs   Hₐ: σ₁² ≠ σ₂²
F = larger sample variance / smaller sample variance
Sample variance = Σ(x − x̄)² / df
Numerator df = df of the larger variance (n₁ − 1); denominator df = n₂ − 1
Also called the variance ratio testAlways put the higher variance on top, so F ≥ 1

Chi-Square (χ²) Tests — Dr Krishan Pandey

≈— marks · 4 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

What chi-square is for

★★★★★

In plain words: chi-square works with counts in categories (how many chose red, blue…). It asks: are the counts we observed close to the counts we would expect if nothing special were going on?

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) — Dr Krishan Pandey

≈— marks · 4 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

What ANOVA does

★★★★★

In plain words: a t-test compares two means. ANOVA compares three or more means at once. It splits the total spread of the scores into two parts: spread between the groups (are the group means far apart?) and spread within the groups (how much people in the same group differ). If between-group spread is large compared to within-group spread, the groups really differ.

F = MSB / MSW
MSB = SSB / df_B      df_B = K − 1        (K = number of groups)
MSW = SSW / df_W      df_W = N_T − K      (N_T = total subjects)
SST = SSB + SSW       df_T = N_T − 1
SSB = (ΣX₁)²/n₁ + (ΣX₂)²/n₂ + (ΣX₃)²/n₃ − (ΣX_T)²/N_T
SSW = [ΣX₁² − (ΣX₁)²/n₁] + [ΣX₂² − (ΣX₂)²/n₂] + [ΣX₃² − (ΣX₃)²/n₃]
SST = ΣX_T² − (ΣX_T)²/N_T
One independent variable → one-way ANOVA; two → two-way ANOVAH₀: all means equal; Hₐ: 'not H₀' — some or all means differ

Statistical Process Control (control charts)

≈— marks · 3 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

In control vs out of control

★★★★★

In plain words: a factory process always varies a little by chance (random variation). If something specific goes wrong (a machine slips), that is an assignable cause — it can be found and fixed. A process with only random variation is in control.

Out of control → reduce variation and bring the measurements back within limits

x̄ chart (for process means)

★★★★★
Take k samples of size n; x̿ = grand mean of all nk values; s = SD of all nk values
Centreline = x̿
LCL = x̿ − 3s/√n        UCL = x̿ + 3s/√n
A sample mean outside the limits → process may be out of control
Based on the CLT: almost all x̄ fall within μ ± 3σ/√n

p chart (for proportion defective)

★★★★★
p̄ = Σp̂ᵢ / k   (average of the k sample proportions)
Centreline = p̄
LCL = p̄ − 3√(p̄(1 − p̄)/n)        UCL = p̄ + 3√(p̄(1 − p̄)/n)
A sample proportion outside the limits → process may be out of control

Ten things beginners get wrong

★★★★★
  • Reject H₀ when p is small (≤ α), not large
  • 'Do not reject' ≠ 'H₀ proved true'
  • Type I = rejecting a true H₀ (α); Type II = keeping a false H₀ (β)
  • Sample SD divides by n − 1; population SD by N
  • Standard error = σ/√n — it shrinks as n grows
  • Stratified samples every stratum; cluster takes all members of some clusters
  • χ² needs counts (nominal data), not means; it is non-parametric
  • ANOVA tells you that means differ; post hoc tests tell you which
  • Two-tailed 5% uses 1.96; one-tailed 5% uses 1.645
  • CLT: sample means are roughly normal when n ≥ 30, even from a skewed population
