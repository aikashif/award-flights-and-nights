Problem formulation: management problem vs research problem

★★★★★

In plain words: the manager asks 'what should I do?' The researcher turns it into 'what do I need to know?' Getting this translation right is the most important step.

Action-orientedInformation-oriented

Should the advertising campaign be changed?Measure how effective the current campaign is
Should the price be raised?Estimate price elasticity of demand and the effect of price changes on sales and profit
Symptom vs problem: falling sales is a symptom; the problem may be a new competitor, poor service or wrong price

Measurement and scaling: basic ideas

★★★★★

In plain words: measurement = giving numbers to characteristics by a rule (1 = male, 2 = female). Scaling = placing objects on a continuum (very unhappy … very happy). Your notes cover the four scale levels (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio); this chapter covers the scaling techniques.

Scale level (recap)What you can sayAllowed statistics
NominalCategories onlyCount, mode, chi-square
OrdinalOrderMedian, percentiles, rank correlation
IntervalEqual gaps, no true zeroMean, SD, correlation, t, ANOVA
RatioTrue zeroAll statistics, incl. ratios

Comparative scaling techniques

★★★★★

In plain words: respondents compare objects directly with each other. Results are ordinal (relative), not absolute.

TechniqueHowExample / note
Paired comparisonChoose one of two objects, for every pairn brands → n(n − 1)/2 pairs: 5 brands = 10 pairs; transitivity check
Rank orderRank all objects togetherRank 5 shampoos 1–5; quick, realistic; n − 1 decisions
Constant sumShare a fixed total (e.g. 100 points) across itemsBrand attributes: price 40, quality 35, design 25; gives ratio-like data
Q-sortSort many statements into piles by criteriaUsed for large numbers of items (60–140)
Pros: easy to understand, small differences show up · Cons: ordinal only; results can't be generalised beyond the objects compared

Multi-item scales and measurement error

★★★★★
A construct (e.g. customer satisfaction) is measured with several items, then summed: develop theory → generate items → reduce items (expert judgement, pretest) → check reliability and validity → final scale
Observed score X_O = X_T (true score) + X_S (systematic error) + X_R (random error)
Reliability: low X_R ; Validity: low X_S and X_R  (X_O = X_T)

Reliability and validity

★★★★★

In plain words: reliable = gives the same result each time. Valid = measures what it should. A bathroom scale that always shows 2 kg too much is reliable but not valid.

Reliability typeHow it is checked
Test–retestSame scale, same people, two times; correlate
Alternative formsTwo equivalent versions; correlate
Internal consistency: split-halfSplit items in two halves; correlate
Internal consistency: Cronbach's alphaAverage of all split-half coefficients; 0–1; ≥ 0.6 acceptable (≥ 0.7 often required)
Validity typeMeaning
Content (face) validityExperts judge whether items cover the construct
Criterion validityAgrees with a criterion: concurrent (same time) or predictive (future)
Construct validityMeasures the theoretical construct: convergent (correlates with related measures), discriminant (not with unrelated), nomological (fits theory)
Valid ⇒ reliable · Reliable does not ⇒ valid · Unreliable ⇒ not validAlso: generalisability — can results extend beyond this sample and setting?

Point & Interval Estimates (Confidence Intervals)

≈Module 3 · Classes 9–10 marks · 7 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

Point vs interval estimates

★★★★★

In plain words: a point estimate is one number used as a best guess for a population value (sample mean 50 → guess μ = 50). An interval estimate gives a range that probably contains the true value, with a stated confidence level (e.g. 95%).

Population parameterPoint estimatorStandard error (SE)
Mean μSample mean x̄σ/√n (or s/√n)
Proportion pSample proportion p̂ = x/n√(p̂q̂/n), q̂ = 1 − p̂
Difference μ₁ − μ₂x̄₁ − x̄₂√(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂)
Difference p₁ − p₂p̂₁ − p̂₂√(p̂₁q̂₁/n₁ + p̂₂q̂₂/n₂)
Variance σ²s² (divide by n − 1)—
Quality of a good estimatorMeaning
UnbiasedIts average over all samples equals the parameter (E(x̄) = μ)
EfficientSmallest standard error among unbiased estimators
ConsistentGets closer to the parameter as n grows
SufficientUses all the information in the sample

General form of a confidence interval

★★★★★
CI = point estimate ± (critical value × standard error)
Margin of error E = critical value × SE
z values: 90% → 1.645 ; 95% → 1.96 ; 99% → 2.576
Meaning of 95%: if we took many samples and built a CI from each, about 95% of them would contain the true μWider interval when: higher confidence, smaller n, bigger σLink to tests: if a two-tailed 5% test value lies outside the 95% CI → reject H₀
Trap Do not say 'there is a 95% probability that μ is in this interval' — μ is fixed; the interval is what varies.

CI for a mean: σ known or large sample (z)

★★★★★
x̄ ± z × σ/√n   (n ≥ 30: use s for σ)
n = 36, x̄ = 50, σ = 12, 95%
SE = 12/√36 = 2 ; E = 1.96 × 2 = 3.92
CI = 50 ± 3.92 = (46.08, 53.92)
Finite population (n/N > 5%): multiply SE by √[(N − n)/(N − 1)]

SE = 8/4 = 2 ; E = 2.131 × 2 = 4.262
CI = 72 ± 4.26 = (67.74, 76.26)

CI for a proportion

★★★★★
p̂ ± z × √(p̂q̂/n)   (need np̂ and nq̂ ≥ 5)
120 of 400 customers prefer brand A → p̂ = 0.30
SE = √(0.30 × 0.70 / 400) = 0.0229 ; E = 1.96 × 0.0229 = 0.0449

CIs for differences (analysis of differences)

★★★★★

In plain words: to compare two groups, build a CI for the difference. If the interval does not include 0, the groups really differ.

Means, large samples: (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± z √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂)
x̄₁ 85, s₁ 10, n₁ 50 ; x̄₂ 80, s₂ 12, n₂ 60
SE = √(100/50 + 144/60) = √4.4 = 2.098 ; E = 1.96 × 2.098 = 4.11
CI = 5 ± 4.11 = (0.89, 9.11) → excludes 0 → significant difference
Means, small samples (pooled): (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± t(n₁ + n₂ − 2) × s_p √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)
Proportions: (p̂₁ − p̂₂) ± z √(p̂₁q̂₁/n₁ + p̂₂q̂₂/n₂)
City A 80/200 = 0.40, city B 60/200 = 0.30
SE = √(0.24/200 + 0.21/200) = 0.0474 ; E = 0.093
CI = 0.10 ± 0.093 = (0.007, 0.193) → excludes 0 → A higher
Paired data: CI = D̄ ± t(n − 1) × s_D/√n

Sample size for estimation

★★★★★
Mean: n = (z σ / E)²
σ = 12, E = 2, 95% → (1.96 × 12 / 2)² = 138.3 → 139 (always round up)
Proportion: n = z² p q / E²
No prior p → use p = 0.5 (largest n)
E = 0.03, 95% → 1.96² × 0.25 / 0.0009 = 1,067.1 → 1,068

t-Tests in Full, Power of a Test & Degrees of Freedom

≈Module 3 · Classes 7–13 marks · 5 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

Two independent samples t-test (pooled variance)

★★★★★

In plain words: compare the means of two separate small groups (n < 30) when population SDs are unknown. If we can assume equal population variances, we pool the two sample variances into one estimate.

H₀: μ₁ = μ₂   Hₐ: μ₁ ≠ μ₂ (or > / <)
s_p² = [(n₁ − 1)s₁² + (n₂ − 1)s₂²] / (n₁ + n₂ − 2)
t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / [s_p √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)]      df = n₁ + n₂ − 2
Worked: method A n₁ = 10, x̄₁ = 20, s₁ = 4 ; method B n₂ = 12, x̄₂ = 17, s₂ = 5 ; α = 5% two-tailed
s_p² = (9 × 16 + 11 × 25) / 20 = 419 / 20 = 20.95 → s_p = 4.577
SE = 4.577 × √(1/10 + 1/12) = 4.577 × 0.428 = 1.960
t = (20 − 17) / 1.960 = 1.53 ; df = 20 → t crit = 2.086
|1.53| < 2.086 → do not reject H₀: no significant difference
95% CI = 3 ± 2.086 × 1.960 = (−1.09, 7.09) → includes 0 (same conclusion)
Check equal variances first with the F-test (larger s² / smaller s²)Unequal variances → Welch's t: t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂)/√(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂) with adjusted (smaller) dfAssumptions: independent random samples, roughly normal populations
Trap Two independent groups ≠ paired data. Same people measured twice → use the paired t-test.

Paired (dependent) t-test: worked

★★★★★

In plain words: when each value in one group is linked to one in the other (same person before/after, matched pairs), test the differences. This removes person-to-person variation and is more powerful.

D = after − before ; D̄ = ΣD/n ; s_D = √[Σ(D − D̄)²/(n − 1)]
t = D̄ / (s_D/√n)     df = n − 1
Training: 6 employees, scores after − before: 3, 5, −1, 4, 2, 5
D̄ = 18/6 = 3 ; deviations 0, 2, −4, 1, −1, 2 → squares sum = 26
s_D = √(26/5) = 2.280 ; SE = 2.280/√6 = 0.931
t = 3 / 0.931 = 3.22 ; df 5, two-tailed 5% crit = 2.571
3.22 > 2.571 → reject H₀: training changed scores
(one-tailed 'improved?' crit 2.015 → also reject)

Power of a test

★★★★★

In plain words: power = the chance the test catches a real effect = P(reject H₀ when H₀ is false) = 1 − β. Low power means real differences are missed (Type II errors).

Worked: H₀: μ = 100 vs Hₐ: μ > 100 ; σ = 15, n = 36 → SE = 2.5 ; α = 5% (z = 1.645)
Reject H₀ if x̄ > 100 + 1.645 × 2.5 = 104.11
If true μ = 106: β = P(x̄ ≤ 104.11 | μ = 106) = P(z ≤ (104.11 − 106)/2.5) = P(z ≤ −0.755) = 0.225
Power = 1 − 0.225 = 0.775
ChangeEffect on power

★★★★★
