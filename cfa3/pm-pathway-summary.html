P1 Index-Based Equity Strategies

≈4% of PM marks · 4 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

Factor-based vs market-cap indexing

★★★★★

In plain words: a normal index fund buys every stock in proportion to its size (market cap). A factor-based fund instead tilts toward stocks with a chosen trait — cheap, small, high momentum — because the investor believes that trait will earn more.

CAPM = one factor (market beta); multifactor models = severalFama–French (2015) five factors: market risk premium · size · book-to-market (value) · operating profitability · investment intensity (asset growth)Common factors: growth, value, size, yield, momentum, quality, volatility
TypeExamples
Return-orientedDividend yield, momentum, fundamentally weighted (dividends, sales, income)
Risk-orientedVolatility weighting (weights = 1/volatility), minimum-variance (Markowitz with constraints)
Diversification-orientedEqual weighting, maximum diversification (max ratio of weighted-average stock volatility to portfolio volatility)
Factor funds are rules-based, transparent → 'passive', but tilting is a form of active managementCheaper than active management, dearer than cap-weighted index fundsTransparency lets others copy → may erode the extra returnMultiple benchmarks (factor + cap-weighted) can raise tracking errorRisk-oriented: simple, reduce risk, but rely on past data
Trap A small-cap, high-P/E (growth) strategy is return-oriented, not risk-oriented.

Tracking error: causes, control, attribution

★★★★★

In plain words: tracking error is how much the fund's return wobbles around the index return (SD of the return differences). For an index fund, low tracking error — not high excess return — shows skill.

Causes: management fees · commissions · sampling · intraday trading (index uses closing prices) · cash dragControl: trade-off between replication cost and sampling error; use index futures to equitise cashAttribution analysis finds the sources of tracking errorSecurities lending income offsets fees → lowers tracking errorIndex managers still owe a fiduciary duty to vote proxies; many use proxy-voting services

Fundamental vs quantitative approaches

★★★★★

In plain words: both try to beat a benchmark. A fundamental manager decides by judgment after studying companies. A quantitative manager decides by rules built from data. The difference is how the buy decision is made, not the tools used.

FundamentalQuantitative
StyleSubjective, analyst discretionObjective, systematic rules from data
FocusCompany: business model, management, valuationFactors across many stocks
PortfolioFewer, larger, high-conviction positionsMany small positions; optimiser maximises alpha / IR
Main riskCompany level: intrinsic value wrong, or mispricing never correctsPortfolio level: factors stop working
RebalancingContinuous, by opinionFixed intervals (monthly, quarterly), by rules
Trap Screening for low P/B can be used by both types. What matters is how the final decision is made.

Top-down strategies

★★★★★
  • Macro, policy and market-level views; implement with broad ETFs and derivatives
  • Dimensions: country/geography · industry sector · volatility (VIX futures, variance swaps, straddles) · thematic (cloud, blockchain, clean energy; or short-term like a political vote)
  • Blend: top-down sets country/sector weights, bottom-up picks stocks; or bottom-up portfolio + top-down derivatives overlay to remove unintended macro bets
  • 'Smart beta' = rotating into factors expected to outperform — a top-down idea
Trap A manager hunting GARP among individual S&P 500 stocks is bottom-up, even if the universe is an index.

Factor-based active strategies & the hedged portfolio

★★★★★

In plain words: a factor is a stock trait linked to returns (small size, cheapness). To measure a factor, rank stocks by it, buy the best slice and short the worst slice, and track that long/short portfolio.

Hedged portfolio (Fama–French): rank universe by factor → split into quantiles (deciles/quintiles)
→ long best quantile, short worst → track return = factor return
Value factor: long HIGH book-to-market (cheap), short LOW book-to-market (expensive)
Drawback of hedged portfolio approach
1Ignores middle quantiles
2Assumes a linear factor–return link
3Correlated factors give less diversification than it seems
4Needs short selling
5Not 'pure' — has exposures to other factors
Rewarded factors = persistent premium; avoid unrewarded ones; factor must make intuitive sense (else data-mined)Factor-mimicking portfolio: dollar-neutral, exposure 1 to one factor, 0 to others — hard to build (liquidity, shorting)Long-only investors tilt toward factors; small tilts = enhanced indexingFactor timing / style rotation: regress factor returns on drivers (e.g. rate surprises)
Rate-surprise regression: current month beta coefficient −0.5 (high beta underperforms after upward surprise)
Next month: size −1.0 (small caps underperform), value negative, beta ≈ +0.7 → timing of the beta effect needs study
Trap Using quartiles instead of deciles is not an error. Buying low book-to-market and shorting high is the error.

★★★★★

Sources of active return & the building blocks

★★★★★

In plain words: active return = portfolio return − benchmark return. It is positive if you overweight the winners. It comes from three places: different exposure to rewarded factors, true skill (alpha), and plain luck from concentrated bets.

Active return = Σ(βₚᵢ − βᵦᵢ) × Factor return ᵢ   +   α (skill)   +   ε (idiosyncratic = luck)
Building blockMeaningLink to return source
1. Factor weightingsOver/underweight rewarded factors (market, size, value, momentum, liquidity) vs benchmark; static exposure no longer counts as alphaLong-term factor exposure
2. Alpha skillsFactor timing (rewarded or unrewarded: sectors, regions, commodities), security selectionMispricing → alpha
3. Position sizingBalance conviction vs idiosyncratic risk; more, smaller positions → lower riskMostly idiosyncratic risk
4. Breadth of expertiseTies them together — more independent decisionsConsistency

Fundamental law of active management

★★★★★
E(R_A) = IC × √BR × σ_A × TC
IC = information coefficient (skill), BR = breadth (independent decisions per year),
σ_A = active risk, TC = transfer coefficient (how well insights reach the portfolio)
Example: 10 decisions/month → BR = 120; IC 0.1, σ_A 5%, TC 0.5
E(R_A) = 0.1 × √120 × 5% × 0.5 = 2.74%
One factor, one metric → decisions not independent → low breadthShorting / fewer constraints raise TC → higher IR
Trap Convert breadth to annual: 10 a month = 120 a year.

Risk budgeting: absolute risk

★★★★★

In plain words: a risk budget hands out the total risk you are willing to take across positions or factors, like a spending budget.

4 steps: choose measure (absolute for total-return mandates, relative for index-beating) → understand risk sources → set budget → allocate
Contribution of asset i to variance: CVᵢ = Σⱼ wᵢ wⱼ Cov(i,j) = wᵢ × Cov(i, portfolio)
Asset A (w 0.20, var 0.05): 0.2×0.2×0.05 + 0.2×0.3×0.0067 + 0.2×0.5×0.0013 = 0.002532
Portfolio variance = 0.002532 + 0.001998 + 0.002880 = 0.00741 → A's share = 34.2%
Factor contribution: Σⱼ βᵢ βⱼ Cov(Fᵢ,Fⱼ); market: 0.892²×0.00178 + 0.892×(−0.283)×0.00042 = 0.00131
Total variance 0.0374² = 0.0013988 → market explains 93.7%; size −0.00007 → unexplained = 0.00016 = 11.4%
Contribution of a sector/country = its weight × covariance with the portfolioShares can exceed 100% when a factor contributes negatively

Risk budgeting: relative (active) risk

★★★★★
Contribution of asset i to active variance: CAVᵢ = (wₚᵢ − wᵦᵢ) × Σⱼ (wₚⱼ − wᵦⱼ) × Cov(active returns i, j)
Driven by active weight and active risk, not absolute SD50/50 benchmark of two indexes + cash in portfolio: cash's active risk is high (low correlation) → cash = 100% of active variance; the two indexes' active returns correlate −1

Choosing the level of risk

★★★★★

≈14% of PM marks · 8 subtopics · chapter priority ★★★★★

Immunisation & duration basics

★★★★★

In plain words: immunisation means building a bond portfolio that will be worth enough to pay a known future bill, whatever happens to interest rates. The easiest way is one zero-coupon bond maturing on the bill date. Otherwise you match duration, so price risk and reinvestment risk cancel.

Macaulay duration = PV-weighted average time to cash flows; zero-coupon = its maturityIf rates rise: price loss offset by higher reinvestment income (and vice versa) — for one small, immediate, parallel shiftModified duration = Macaulay ÷ (1 + periodic yield) → better for immediate price change; Macaulay → better for immunisation timingUse portfolio statistics from aggregate cash flows (portfolio IRR, duration, dispersion, convexity), not market-value weighted averages; same only if curve is flat; with upward slope the cash-flow IRR and duration are higherGoal = earn the initial portfolio IRR, not average YTM
%ΔValue ≈ −MD × ΔY + ½ × C × (ΔY)²
BPV (PVBP, 'value of an 01') = MD × Value × 0.0001

Single-liability immunisation rules & structural risk

★★★★★
1. PV assets ≥ PV liability
2. Macaulay duration of assets = liability due date
3. Minimise convexity (least dispersion of asset cash flows around the liability)
4. Rebalance regularly (trade-off with transaction costs)

Credit risk, spreads and the credit cycle

★★★★★

In plain words: a credit spread is the extra yield a risky bond pays over a safe government bond. It pays you for two risks: the issuer may not pay (credit risk) and you may not be able to sell easily (liquidity risk).

Expected loss = POD × LGD ; LGD = 1 − recovery rate
CVA = PV of Σ(POD × LGD × expected exposure) → risky bond = risk-free bond − CVA
Simple fair spread ≈ POD × LGD
First lien spread 1.5%, recovery 50% → POD = 1.5%/0.5 = 3%
Second lien recovery 40% → spread = 3% × 0.6 = 1.8%
IG = BBB and above (main risk = credit migration/downgrade); HY = BB and below (main risk = default)Credit spread curve = spread vs maturity; upward = defaults more likely laterCycle: peak → spreads rising, curve upward sloping; recession → IG flattens, HY inverts (near-term default risk); HY moves more sharplyRates and spreads move oppositely → HY bond price reacts less to rate changes: empirical duration < analytical duration

The illiquidity premium

★★★★★

In plain words: investors want extra return for money they cannot get back quickly. The longer the lock-up, the bigger the premium.

Option view: price of illiquid asset = price of same marketable asset − value of a put (the right to sell you gave up)
