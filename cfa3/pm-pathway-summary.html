Single-factor vs multifactor models (Fama-French five)

★★★★★

In plain words: a 'factor' is a common driver of stock returns. CAPM says only one factor matters — the market (measured by beta). Multifactor models say returns depend on exposure to several factors.

Fama-French (2015) factorHow it is measured
1. Market risk premiumBeta (standardised covariance with market return)
2. Firm sizeMarket value of equity
3. Book-to-marketShareholders' equity ÷ market value of equity
4. Operating profitabilityOperating income ÷ beginning shareholders' equity
5. Investment intensityGrowth rate of total assets
Other common factors: growth, value, size, yield, momentum, quality, volatilityFactor indexes let investors add to or replace a cap-weighted index based on their view of future factor returnsUsually packaged as ETFs

Factor-based strategies: passive or active?

★★★★★
Passive side: rules-based, transparent, replicableActive side: choosing to emphasise some factors over the market portfolio is an active decision; managers may exploit out-of-favour factorsCosts: lower than traditional active funds, higher than large cap-weighted index funds (higher fees and commissions)Transparency lets others copy the strategy → their buying moves prices → extra factor return can shrink or disappearManagers often use several benchmarks (factor + cap-weighted) → can raise tracking error
Trap A factor index fund is passive in implementation but active in factor choice.

Tracking error: causes and control

★★★★★

In plain words: tracking error = standard deviation of (portfolio return − index return). For an index manager, low tracking error is the skill — the goal is to match, not beat.

Tracking error = SD(R_portfolio − R_index)
CauseWhy
Management feesIndex has no fees
Trade commissionsIndex trades for free
SamplingNot all stocks held
Intraday tradingIndex uses closing prices
Cash dragCash held for flows; lowers return in rising markets, helps in falling markets
Control = trade-off: full replication's higher trading costs vs sampling's tracking errorMust still trade for inflows, outflows and dividend reinvestmentIndex futures on idle cash reduce cash dragQuiz: tracking error is a better skill measure than excess return because the goal is to match the index

★★★★★

Factor-based strategies: rewarded factors & hedged portfolio

★★★★★

In plain words: a factor is a stock trait linked to returns (e.g. small size, high book-to-market). Rewarded factors earn a long-term risk premium; unrewarded ones do not. A factor must make intuitive sense — otherwise heavy backtesting finds false patterns.

Hedged portfolio approach (Fama-French):
1. Rank universe by the factor (size → market cap)
2. Split into quantiles (deciles 10%, quintiles 20%, quartiles 25%)
3. Long best quantile, short worst (size: long smallest 10%, short largest 10%)
4. Track the long/short return = the factor's return
DrawbackMeaning
Middle quantiles ignoredBest stocks may sit in the middle
Assumes linear factor–return linkNon-linear effects missed
False diversificationSeveral highly correlated factors
Needs short sellingNot always allowed
Not a pure factorHas exposures to other factors
Factor-mimicking portfolio: theoretical, dollar-neutral, exposure of 1 to the chosen factor and 0 to others; hard to build (liquidity, shorting)Long-only investors tilt toward factors; modest tilts = low tracking error = enhanced indexingReal value added = finding factors that predict the future
Trap Value factor: go long high book-to-market (cheap), short low B/M. Using quartiles instead of deciles is not an error (quiz C).

Factor timing (style rotation) worked example

★★★★★

In plain words: some factors work better in some conditions. Regress factor returns on a suspected driver to find when to hold each factor.

Driver: ISurprise = actual central bank rate − rate priced in Eurodollar futures the day before (high = hawkish surprise)
Current month: beta (market) coefficient −0.5 → high-beta stocks lose on upward surprise; size and value no signal
Next month: size −1.0 (small caps underperform after hawkish surprise); value also negative; beta ≈ +0.7
Issue: beta sign flips (− now, + next month) → need to know how long the effect lasts before trading on it

Statistical arbitrage & market microstructure

★★★★★

In plain words: stat arb uses price and volume data to profit when related prices drift apart and then come back (mean reversion).

Pairs trading: two historically correlated stocks in one industry
Spread = ln(P_A / P_B)  (log gives a more stable signal)
Spread > moving average + 2 SD → SELL the spread (short A, long B)
Spread < moving average − 2 SD → BUY the spread
Profit if the gap closes; risk = relationship broken for good
Market microstructure arbitrage: supply/demand imbalances lasting milliseconds; needs limit order book analysis and high-frequency trading (quiz B)Stat arb and event-driven are usually quantitative but may include judgment

Event-driven strategies & risk arbitrage

★★★★★

In plain words: event-driven managers profit from mispricing around corporate events: M&A, earnings, restructuring, buybacks, special dividends, spin-offs. Unlike activists, they do not cause the event.

Backtesting: factor scores and information coefficient (IC)

★★★★★

In plain words: the IC is the correlation between today's factor scores and next period's returns across stocks. Positive and significant = the factor predicts.

Standardised factor score (z) = (stock value − mean) / SD
E/P 7%, mean 3%, SD 5% → (7 − 3)/5 = 0.8
Pearson IC = correlation(factor score, subsequent return) — monthly 5–6% is very strong; hurt by outliers
Spearman Rank IC = correlation of the ranks → robust to outliers
9-stock example: Pearson IC −0.99% (distorted by stock A: negative score, +10.06% return)
Spearman Rank IC +40% → high E/P does predict
Long top 3 (G, H, I): (3.02 + 3.05 + 5.20)/3 = 3.76%
Short bottom 3 (A, B, C): (10.06 − 0.6 − 0.5)/3 = 2.99%
Long/short = 0.77%; sample too small → test more data and periods
Multifactor model: weight factors by judgment or mean-variance optimisation; a factor adds little if correlated with othersEvaluate: out-of-sample tests; returns plus VaR and maximum drawdownPortfolio construction: commercial risk models (variances, correlations) and trading costs (explicit + market impact) — same return → choose lower cost
Trap Predictive power uses subsequent returns, not contemporaneous. Outliers → Spearman (quiz B).

Active return and its three sources

★★★★★

In plain words: active return = portfolio return − benchmark return. It is positive if you overweight winners and underweight losers. Active portfolios aim to beat the benchmark after all costs.

R_A = Σ (wₚᵢ − wᵦᵢ) × Rᵢ

Portfolio statistics from aggregate cash flows

★★★★★

In plain words: for liability work, compute yield, duration and convexity from the portfolio's combined cash flows, not as market-value-weighted averages of each bond. (You will not compute these on the exam — understand how to use them.)

1. List every portfolio cash flow by six-month period
2. Add cash flows in each period
3. Portfolio IRR = rate that equates them to current market value
4. PV each cash flow at the IRR (sum = market value)
5. Weight w = PV ÷ total PV
6. Macaulay duration = Σ t × w (÷ 2 for years)
7. Dispersion = weighted variance of timing around duration
8. Convexity = Σ [t (t + 1) w] ÷ (1 + periodic IRR)²
Flat curve: same answer either wayUpward-sloping curve: portfolio IRR and duration are higher than the averages (long bonds count for longer)Goal = earn the initial portfolio IRR, not average YTMAverages are used in practice as approximations — they add errorDispersion and convexity show exposure to structural risk from curve shifts and twists
